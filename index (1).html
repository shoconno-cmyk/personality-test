A curious peek, not serious psychometrics

41 AI Models Meet the Big Five Personality Test

Shane O'Connor · 27 October 2026 · Data: Artificial Analysis PersonalityBench

I scored 41 AI models on the Big Five personality traits, then found the data only supports about two real dimensions, and one of them looks like each lab's house style.

This is a curious peek, not a validated psychometric instrument. I wanted to see what happens when you hold a new kind of writer up against the most studied model of human personality. The leaderboards came out the way you might expect. The structure underneath them did not.

Why the Big Five

The Big Five grew out of language. In 1936, Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert combed the English dictionary for every word that describes a person, about 18,000 of them. Their bet, the lexical hypothesis, was that the traits that matter most end up encoded in everyday words.

Decades of factor analysis on trait ratings kept finding the same five clusters, first in Tupes and Christal's US Air Force research (1961), then in Norman's replication (1963). Lewis Goldberg named them the Big Five in 1990. Paul Costa and Robert McCrae built the NEO PI-R questionnaire, and McCrae and John's 1992 overview became the standard reference. Kibeom Lee and Michael Ashton later added a sixth factor, Honesty-Humility, in the HEXACO model.

That origin is why this experiment felt worth trying. AA-PersonalityBench has AI judges describe AI writing in human trait words: friendly, confident, eloquent, sycophantic. It is the lexical approach run in reverse, on a new kind of writer.

How I scored them

The data is a snapshot of Artificial Analysis's AA-PersonalityBench from 27 October 2026: 41 models, 30 writing-style traits, each reported as a percentile rank across the pool. Every model answered 900 questions with no system prompt, so this is each model's default voice. A panel of three AI judges (Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GLM 5.3 Flash) compared answers two at a time, blind.

I assigned the 30 traits to Big Five and HEXACO dimensions by meaning, and locked that mapping before looking at any results. Each trait is used at most once per dimension. Reverse-scored traits are marked (−).

Friendly sits under Extraversion because the NEO PI-R places warmth there. Sycophancy was kept out of Agreeableness on purpose: it fits the Compliance facet but runs against Straightforwardness, so it pulls the scale both ways. Four traits had no clean home and were left out: Natural Sounding, Information Dense, Resource-Specific and Scannable.

Each percentile was converted to a z-score with a rank-based normal transformation (Blom), reverse-scored traits were flipped, and each dimension is the mean of its traits.

What this measures. These are scores for text behaviour, described in a human trait vocabulary. A model that writes in an agreeable pattern is not claimed to be agreeable, and nothing here depends on whether models have inner experience.

The leaderboards

Three of the five scales held together well, and the newest, most polished models filled out nearly every axis. The radars show nine models, at least one per major lab, as each model's rank among all 41.

How well each scale holds together (Cronbach's alpha, where 0.7 or above is respectable): Honesty-Humility 0.91, Openness 0.89, Conscientiousness 0.81, Extraversion 0.64. The locked Agreeableness scale failed at −1.04, so the radars use the repaired version, whose two traits correlate at r = 0.63.

Extraversion is the softest scale because Direct and Friendly pull against each other. Across all 41 models, the same three legacy models (GPT-3.5 Turbo, Llama 4 Maverick and GPT-4o) sit near the bottom of almost every scale.

The first crack: Agreeableness

The locked Agreeableness scale failed outright. In people, pushing back counts against Agreeableness. In these models, Constructive Pushback rises with Sincere (ρ = 0.51) and Emotionally Intelligent (ρ = 0.62), and falls with Sycophantic (ρ = −0.63). The warm models are also the ones that push back, and the people-pleasers are the weaker models. The judges seem to read pushback as honesty, not disagreeableness.

I report the failure as it stands, beside a clearly labelled repair that keeps only Sincere and Emotionally Intelligent. The repair was added after seeing the results, so treat it as exploratory.

The missing trait: Neuroticism

There is no Neuroticism score. The benchmark never puts a model under emotional pressure, so there is no behaviour to score. That is a limit of the measurement, not evidence that models are emotionally stable.

Sidebar, just for fun: Anxious Hedging

I tried a novelty stand-in anyway: Admits Uncertainty, Disclaims Assumptions, Sycophantic and reverse-scored Confident. It does not hold together (alpha 0.12), so it is not a scale. For what it is worth, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol and Muse Spark Open came out most "anxious". The most epistemically careful models look neurotic, which says more about the index than the models.

The twist: two personalities, not five

When the independent reviewer checked the structure, the five scores turned out to be mostly measuring two things. A parallel analysis, which tests each component against random shuffles of the same data, found two components that clearly beat chance (13.5 and 6.0 against chance levels of 3.4 and 2.9) and a borderline third.

Polish, 45% of the variance. Lexically Varied, Pragmatic, Eloquent and Creative load strongly on it, and Sycophantic and Slop load against it. GPT-3.5 Turbo, Llama 4 Maverick and GPT-4o sit at the far left. Without those three it drops to 35%, so part of polish is simply how recent the model is, and part is likely the judges' halo.

House style, 20% of the variance. One end is Direct, Answer First, Admits Uncertainty and Truthful. The other is Resource-Specific, Slop, Metaphorical and Scannable. OpenAI's GPT-6 family and Grok 4.6 and 4.7 sit at the terse end, Gemini and Muse models at the decorative end, and Claude, GLM and Kimi in between. Models from the same lab cluster together, which suggests each lab trains a recognizable voice.

That recasts the leaderboards. Models that topped several Big Five boards were not five-way personalities; they were mostly polished. The Big Five supplied the vocabulary, and the data gave back two dimensions.

What this can and can't say

It can describe how these 41 models write by default, in a vocabulary people already understand. It cannot say what any model is like inside.

  • AI judges rating writing. Every trait comes from AI judges reading single answers, and one of the judges (Claude Sonnet 5.5) is also in the pool. A shared halo across all 30 traits is likely.
  • One default answer. Each model answered each question once, with no system prompt, in English. Long conversations or set personas may look different.
  • Relative to this pool. Every score is a rank among these 41 models as of 27 October 2026. New models will move everyone (like GPT-6.1 Terra, released 26 October). The pool grew from 36 to 41 within a week of launch.
  • Ranks are fuzzy. In a resampling test, Claude Fable 5.1's Honesty-Humility rank ranged from 1st to 6th, and GPT-6.1 Sol's from 2nd to 17th. Read the clusters, not the positions.
  • Five labels, fewer dimensions. The dimensions correlate with each other at 0.5 to 0.7, where human Big Five scales are designed to be fairly independent. The twist section above shows why.
